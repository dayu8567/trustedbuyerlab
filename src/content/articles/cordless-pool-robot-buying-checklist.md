A pool robot can look impressive in a product photo and still be a poor fit for the pool behind your house. The useful questions are less about the longest feature list and more about how the machine will handle your pool shape, the debris you actually get, and the amount of attention you want to give it.

Use this checklist to narrow the market before comparing prices.

## 1. Measure the pool, not just the square footage

Square footage is a starting point, but the robot also has to navigate the shape. A long narrow pool, sharp corners, a kidney shape, a steep slope, or several ledges can all change how effectively a cleaner reaches the surfaces you care about.

Write down:

- The longest and widest dimensions.
- The pool floor type and whether the liner has seams or wrinkles.
- Steps, benches, ledges, main drains, and other obstacles.
- Whether the pool is above-ground or in-ground.

Then check whether the manufacturer describes the cleaner for that construction and surface type. A broad claim such as "large pools" is less useful than guidance that matches your actual layout.

## 2. Decide which surfaces need cleaning

Pool robots commonly differ in whether they are intended for the floor only or also for walls and the waterline. That distinction can matter more than an extra app feature.

Start with the cleaning job that bothers you most:

- **Floor only:** debris settles on the bottom and you handle walls separately.
- **Floor and walls:** the robot needs a navigation and traction system that can climb your wall material.
- **Floor, walls, and waterline:** the cleaner needs to reach the transition at the top of the pool and scrub that band.

If you already brush the waterline often, paying for waterline cleaning may be worthwhile. If your pool has mostly sunken debris and simple surfaces, floor coverage may be the better use of budget.

## 3. Match filtration to the debris

Leaves, sand, pollen, pet hair, and fine dust do not place the same demands on a filter basket. Look for the stated basket capacity, filter type, and cleaning instructions. A large basket reduces the number of interruptions, while a finer filter may be more useful for cloudy residue but require more frequent rinsing.

Think about the worst week of the year, not the cleanest one. A basket that is adequate for a lightly used pool may become a chore during leaf season.

## 4. Treat runtime as a range, not a promise

Published runtime is usually tied to a cleaning mode and a fresh battery. Navigation, climbing, debris load, water temperature, and battery age can all affect the real result. Compare runtime together with pool size and charging time.

Also check how the cleaner is retrieved. A long runtime is not helpful if the machine is difficult to lift from the water or recharge in your storage space.

## 5. Check ownership details before the price

The headline price is only one part of ownership. Confirm the warranty terms, availability of replacement brushes and filters, app requirements, storage dimensions, and whether the manufacturer recommends any specific water chemistry range.

## The short version

Choose the smallest set of features that solves your actual cleaning problem. Pool shape, surface coverage, debris capacity, and easy maintenance should come before optional scheduling features. A simpler cleaner that fits the pool is usually a better purchase than a feature-heavy model that does not.
