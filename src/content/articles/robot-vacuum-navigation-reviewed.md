Robot vacuum navigation labels describe sensing methods, not guaranteed cleaning performance. Lidar, cameras, bumper sensors, and mapping software work together. A product can have sophisticated navigation and still leave a trail of pet hair if the brush, suction path, or maintenance routine is poor.

This review compares the systems through published specifications and ownership requirements. It is not a hands-on test of any model.

## Lidar: a floor plan in the dark

Lidar uses laser distance measurements to build a map of rooms and obstacles. It can operate without room lights because it does not depend on a camera image. That can be useful for scheduled cleaning when the house is dark.

The limitation is what the sensor can see. A low cable, a reflective object, or a soft curtain may not be interpreted as a wall. Lidar also needs enough clearance for the turret. A low cabinet or sofa may block the line of sight and create a map that does not match the room.

## Cameras: context and obstacles

Camera-based navigation can use visual features to recognize rooms, furniture, and obstacles. Some systems add object recognition for cables or pet waste. These features can be useful, but they depend on lighting, processing, and training.

Privacy is part of the specification. Check where images are processed, whether cloud upload is optional, and what happens when the internet is unavailable. A camera robot may require an account even when you intend to use local controls.

## Mapping and zones

A map is only useful when it supports the routine. Look for room segmentation, no-go zones, multi-floor support, and the ability to recover after being moved. Some apps require a first complete run before zones become useful. Others may struggle when furniture changes.

If a robot relies on the charging dock to orient itself, placement matters. The dock should not sit in a high-traffic corner or behind a door. The manual's clearance dimensions are part of the product specification even though they rarely appear on a marketing page.

## Obstacle avoidance is not supervision

No robot should be trusted to navigate every hazard. Cables, liquid, loose rugs, stairs, and pet accidents remain real risks. Stair sensors need a clean surface to work, and cliff detection can be affected by dark flooring or unusual thresholds.

Prepare the room, use physical barriers where necessary, and keep the brush and sensors clean. A robot that maps a room well can still fail when a filter is clogged or a wheel is wrapped in hair.

## What to compare

Compare the sensor package, required lighting, mapping controls, obstacle-avoidance claims, privacy model, replacement parts, and app requirements. Then compare the cleaning hardware that actually removes debris.

The useful conclusion is not "lidar beats camera" or the reverse. The useful question is whether the navigation method fits the rooms, the lighting, the obstacles, and the level of supervision you are prepared to provide.
